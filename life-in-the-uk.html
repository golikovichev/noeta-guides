What a question looks like

One real question from the bank, with the answer and the document it was written from.

Who leads the UK government?

  1. The Prime Minister — correct answer
  2. The monarch
  3. The Speaker of the House of Commons
  4. The Lord Chief Justice

The Prime Minister leads the government and carries ultimate responsibility for its policy and decisions. The monarch is head of state, not head of government: the King appoints the Prime Minister but does not run the country. The Speaker chairs debates in the Commons and stays politically neutral, and the Lord Chief Justice heads the judiciary, which is separate from government.

Source: www.gov.uk/government/how-government-works

How to book

Book it yourself through GOV.UK at least 3 days before the date you want. It costs £50 and you pay each time you sit it. Bring the same original ID you used to book — a copy or a screenshot is refused and the fee is not refunded; your photo is taken at the centre.

Five more questions, with answers

Every question below is a real question from the bank, written from the document shown with it. The exam does not publish its own questions, so these are not exam questions — they are what the published text supports.

1. Which Act of Parliament created the Scottish Parliament?

  1. The Scotland Act 1998 — correct answer
  2. The Act of Union 1707
  3. The Government of Wales Act 1998
  4. The Human Rights Act 1998

Devolution came in 1998, and the Scotland Act of that year opens by simply stating that there shall be a Scottish Parliament. The 1707 union did the opposite - it ended the old Scottish Parliament by merging the two kingdoms. The Government of Wales Act is the parallel statute for Wales, and the Human Rights Act deals with rights, not devolution.

Source: www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1998/46/section/1

2. Which Act brought the rights of the European Convention on Human Rights directly into UK law?

  1. The Human Rights Act 1998 — correct answer
  2. The Equality Act 2010
  3. The Bill of Rights 1689
  4. The Magna Carta

The Human Rights Act 1998 defines the Convention rights and makes them enforceable in UK courts, so a case no longer has to travel to Strasbourg to rely on them. The Equality Act 2010 covers discrimination, and the Bill of Rights and Magna Carta are far older constitutional documents with nothing to do with the Convention, which was written after the Second World War.

Source: www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1998/42/section/1

3. How many jurors decide a criminal trial in England and Wales?

  1. 12 — correct answer
  2. 8
  3. 10
  4. 15

A jury in England and Wales is twelve people. Scotland is the exception in the UK and uses a larger jury for criminal trials, which is why the question names the jurisdiction. Jury service is a duty, not a volunteer role: if you are summoned you must respond.

Source: www.gov.uk/jury-service

4. What does the Government need before it can make new laws or raise new taxes?

  1. A referendum in each nation of the UK
  2. Parliament's agreement — correct answer
  3. The approval of the Supreme Court
  4. A majority vote of the Cabinet

The Government cannot make new laws or raise new taxes without "Parliament's agreement". Parliament represents our interests and makes sure they are taken into account by the Government. The Supreme Court rules on legal disputes rather than approving legislation, and a Cabinet vote has no force in law on its own.

Source: www.parliament.uk/about/how/role/parliament-government/

5. The Central Criminal Court in London is more commonly known by which name?

  1. The Royal Courts of Justice
  2. The Inns of Court
  3. The Old Bailey — correct answer
  4. Westminster Hall

The Central Criminal Court is more commonly known as "The Old Bailey", and it is one of the Crown Court centres in England and Wales. The Royal Courts of Justice house the civil courts, including the Court of Appeal (Civil Division). The Inns of Court are the professional bodies for barristers, and Westminster Hall is part of the Palace of Westminster.

Source: www.judiciary.uk/courts-and-tribunals/crown-court/
